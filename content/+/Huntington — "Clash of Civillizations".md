## Central Thesis

Samuel Huntington argues that in the post-Cold War world, the fundamental source of conflict will not be primarily ideological or economic, but **cultural**. The principal conflicts of global politics will occur between nations and groups of different civilizations, with the fault lines between civilizations becoming the battle lines of the future.

## Evolution of Conflict in the Modern World

Huntington traces how patterns of conflict have evolved through three historical phases:

**Phase 1 (Post-1648 to French Revolution)**: Conflicts were among princes—emperors and monarchs competing to expand bureaucracies, armies, and territories, creating nation-states in the process.

**Phase 2 (French Revolution to WWI)**: As R.R. Palmer noted, "The wars of kings were over; the wars of peoples had begun." Conflict shifted from princes to nations.

**Phase 3 (Post-Russian Revolution to Cold War)**: Conflict became ideological—first among communism, fascism-Nazism, and liberal democracy, then between communism and liberal democracy. These were essentially "Western civil wars" since all ideologies originated in Western civilization.

**Phase 4 (Post-Cold War)**: International politics moves out of its Western phase. Non-Western civilizations are no longer merely objects of history but active movers and shapers of it.

## Definition and Nature of Civilizations

Huntington defines civilization as the highest cultural grouping of people and the broadest level of cultural identity. A civilization is characterized by:

- Common objective elements: language, history, religion, customs, institutions
- Subjective self-identification of people

For example, a resident of Rome may identify as a Roman, Italian, Catholic, Christian, European, and Westerner—with Western civilization being the broadest level of identity.

Huntington identifies **seven or eight major civilizations** in the contemporary world:

1. Western
2. Confucian
3. Japanese
4. Islamic
5. Hindu
6. Slavic-Orthodox
7. Latin American
8. Possibly African

## Six Reasons Why Civilizations Will Clash

**First: Differences are basic and fundamental.** Civilizations differ in history, language, culture, tradition, and most importantly, religion. People of different civilizations have different views on relations between God and man, individual and group, citizen and state, parents and children, husband and wife, and on the relative importance of rights and responsibilities, liberty and authority, equality and hierarchy. For example, Western ideas of individualism, liberalism, constitutionalism, human rights, and separation of church and state often have little resonance in Islamic, Confucian, Japanese, Hindu, or Buddhist cultures.

**Second: The world is becoming smaller.** Increased interactions between peoples of different civilizations intensify civilization consciousness and awareness of differences. Huntington gives the example of how North African immigration to France generates hostility among the French while increasing receptivity to "good" European Catholic Poles. Similarly, as Donald Horowitz noted, "An Ibo may be an Owerri Ibo or an Onitsha Ibo in eastern Nigeria. In Lagos, he is simply an Ibo. In London, he is a Nigerian. In New York, he is an African."

**Third: Economic modernization and social change weaken traditional local identities and the nation-state.** Religion, often in fundamentalist movements, has moved in to fill this gap. These movements are found across religions—Western Christianity, Judaism, Buddhism, Hinduism, and Islam—and are typically led by young, college-educated, middle-class professionals. This "unsecularization of the world" provides a basis for identity that transcends national boundaries.

**Fourth: The dual role of the West.** While the West is at a peak of power, a "return to roots" phenomenon is occurring among non-Western civilizations—the "Asianization" of Japan, "Hinduization" of India, "re-Islamization" of the Middle East. The West's efforts to promote its values as universal produces a backlash.

**Fifth: Cultural characteristics are less mutable than political or economic ones.** In class conflicts, people could choose sides and change sides. In civilizational conflicts, the question is "What are you?"—something that cannot be changed. As Huntington notes, from Bosnia to the Caucasus to Sudan, the wrong answer can mean "a bullet in the head." A person can be half-French and half-Arab, even a citizen of two countries, but it's more difficult to be half-Catholic and half-Muslim.

**Sixth: Economic regionalism is increasing.** However, economic regionalism succeeds only when rooted in common civilization. The European Community rests on European culture and Western Christianity. NAFTA depends on the convergence of Mexican, Canadian, and American cultures. Japan faces difficulties creating a comparable entity in East Asia because it is culturally unique. In contrast, common culture facilitates economic expansion between mainland China and Hong Kong, Taiwan, Singapore, and overseas Chinese communities.

## Fault Lines Between Civilizations

### Europe's Cultural Division

The most significant dividing line in Europe may be the eastern boundary of Western Christianity circa 1500. This line:

- Runs along the boundaries between Finland and Russia, the Baltic states and Russia
- Cuts through Belarus and Ukraine, separating Catholic western Ukraine from Orthodox eastern Ukraine
- Swings westward separating Transylvania from Romania
- Goes through Yugoslavia, separating Croatia and Slovenia from the rest

This coincides with the historic boundary between the Hapsburg and Ottoman empires. Peoples to the north and west shared the experiences of feudalism, Renaissance, Reformation, Enlightenment, French Revolution, and Industrial Revolution—they are generally economically better off and developing stable democracies. Peoples to the east and south belonged to Ottoman or Tsarist empires, were lightly touched by European developments, are generally less economically advanced, and less likely to develop stable democracies. As Huntington puts it, "The Velvet Curtain of culture has replaced the Iron Curtain of ideology."

### Western-Islamic Fault Line

Conflict along this fault line has been ongoing for 1,300 years:

- Arab and Moorish surge west ended at Tours in 732
- Crusades (11th-13th centuries) attempted to bring Christianity to the Holy Land
- Ottoman Turks reversed the balance (14th-17th centuries), capturing Constantinople and twice besieging Vienna
- Western colonial control of North Africa and Middle East (19th-early 20th centuries)
- Post-WWII conflicts: Arab nationalism, Islamic fundamentalism, Arab-Israeli wars, Algeria, Lebanon, Libya, Iran, terrorism, Gulf War

Huntington notes that the crescent-shaped Islamic bloc "from the bulge of Africa to central Asia, has bloody borders," with violence occurring between Muslims and Orthodox Serbs, Jews in Israel, Hindus in India, Buddhists in Burma, and Catholics in the Philippines.

## The Kin-Country Syndrome: Civilization Rallying

Huntington introduces the concept of "civilization rallying" or the "kin-country syndrome"—groups or states belonging to one civilization naturally rally support from other members when involved in conflict with people from a different civilization.

**Gulf War Example**: While few Muslim governments overtly supported Saddam Hussein, many Arab elites privately cheered him, and he was highly popular among Arab publics. Islamic fundamentalist movements universally supported Iraq rather than Western-backed governments. Saddam explicitly invoked Islamic appeal, framing it as "the West against Islam." Iran's Ayatollah Khamenei called for holy war against the West. King Hussein of Jordan argued it was "a war against all Arabs and all Muslims."

**Caucasus Example**: Armenian military successes stimulated Turkey to support its religious and ethnic brethren in Azerbaijan. Turkish officials spoke of being "under pressure" with newspapers full of atrocity photos. President Özal threatened Turkey would "show its fangs." Meanwhile, after the Soviet Union's collapse, Russian troops fought alongside Christian Armenians.

**Bosnia Example**: Germany led European Community recognition of Catholic Slovenia and Croatia. The Vatican extended recognition even before the EC. Western publics sympathized with Bosnian Muslims suffering under Serbs, though less attention was given to Croatian attacks on Muslims. Islamic governments and groups castigated the West for not defending Bosnians. Iran supplied weapons and men, and by 1993, up to 4,000 Muslims from over two dozen Islamic countries were fighting in Bosnia. Saudi Arabia supplied substantial funding. A Saudi editor observed: "The war in Bosnia-Herzegovina has become the emotional equivalent of the fight against fascism in the Spanish Civil War."

## The West Versus the Rest

The West is at an extraordinary peak of power:

- Its superpower opponent (USSR) has disappeared
- Military conflict among Western states is unthinkable
- Western military power is unrivaled
- It dominates international political, security, and economic institutions
- Global decisions are made by a directorate of the U.S., Britain, and France (political/security) and U.S., Germany, and Japan (economic)

The phrase "the world community" has become a euphemism to give global legitimacy to actions reflecting Western interests. Through institutions like the IMF, the West promotes its economic interests and imposes policies it thinks appropriate. As Georgy Arbatov characterized it, IMF officials are "neo-Bolsheviks who love expropriating other people's money, imposing undemocratic and alien rules of economic and political conduct and stifling economic freedom."

Western concepts—individualism, liberalism, constitutionalism, human rights, equality, liberty, rule of law, democracy, free markets, separation of church and state—often have little resonance in other civilizations. As one study of 100 comparative value studies concluded: "the values that are most important in the West are least important worldwide."

Non-Western responses generally take three forms:

1. **Isolation**: Like Burma and North Korea, attempting to insulate from Western "corruption" (rarely pursued exclusively due to high costs)
    
2. **Bandwagoning**: Attempting to join the West and accept its values and institutions
    
3. **Balancing**: Developing economic and military power, cooperating with other non-Western societies against the West, while preserving indigenous values—to modernize but not Westernize
    

## Torn Countries

Torn countries have cultural homogeneity but are divided over whether they belong to one civilization or another. Their leaders wish to join the West, but their history, culture, and traditions are non-Western.

**Turkey**: The prototypical torn country. Leaders have followed Atatürk's tradition, defining Turkey as modern, secular, and Western. Turkey allied with the West in NATO and the Gulf War and applied for EC membership. However, elements of Turkish society support Islamic revival and argue Turkey is fundamentally Middle Eastern Muslim. Meanwhile, the West refuses to accept Turkey—it won't become an EC member because, as President Özal said, "we are Muslim and they are Christian and they don't say that." Having rejected Mecca and been rejected by Brussels, Turkey may look to Tashkent as leader of a revived Turkic civilization involving seven countries from Greece to China.

**Mexico**: During the past decade, Mexico stopped defining itself by opposition to the United States and is attempting to imitate it and join NAFTA. Mexican leaders are redefining Mexican identity through fundamental economic reforms leading to political change. A Salinas adviser told Huntington: "Exactly! That's precisely what we are trying to do [change Mexico from a Latin American to North American country], but of course we could never say so publicly." Like Turkey's leaders making gestures to Islam (Özal's Mecca pilgrimage), Mexico's leaders make gestures to those viewing Mexico as Latin American (Salinas's Ibero-American summit).

**Russia**: The most important torn country globally. The question of whether Russia is part of the West or leader of a distinct Slavic-Orthodox civilization has been recurring in Russian history. President Yeltsin is adopting Western principles and seeking to make Russia a "normal" country and part of the West. However, Russian elite and public are divided. Sergei Stankevich argues Russia should reject the "Atlanticist" course and instead emphasize Turkic and Muslim connections and promote "an appreciable redistribution of our resources, our options, our ties, and our interests in favor of Asia." More extreme dissidents voice nationalist, anti-Western, anti-Semitic views. A 1992 opinion survey in European Russia revealed 40 percent had positive attitudes toward the West and 36 percent had negative attitudes.

To successfully redefine civilization identity, a torn country must meet three requirements:

1. Political and economic elite must be supportive and enthusiastic
2. Public must be willing to acquiesce
3. Dominant groups in the recipient civilization must be willing to embrace the convert

All three largely exist for Mexico, the first two largely exist for Turkey, but it's unclear any exist for Russia joining the West.

## The Confucian-Islamic Connection

Almost without exception, Western countries are reducing military power. China, North Korea, and several Middle Eastern states, however, are significantly expanding military capabilities through arms imports and indigenous development. This has produced the emergence of "Weapons States"—and the Weapons States are not Western states.

The conflict focuses on nuclear, chemical, and biological weapons, ballistic missiles, and sophisticated delivery systems. The West promotes nonproliferation as a universal norm. Non-Western nations assert their right to acquire whatever weapons they think necessary for security. They've absorbed the truth of the Indian defense minister's lesson from the Gulf War: "Don't fight the United States unless you have nuclear weapons."

**China's Military Expansion**: Buoyed by spectacular economic development, China is rapidly increasing military spending and modernizing its armed forces. It's purchasing weapons from former Soviet states, developing long-range missiles, tested a one-megaton nuclear device in 1992, developing power-projection capabilities, acquiring aerial refueling technology, and trying to purchase an aircraft carrier.

**Weapons and Technology Flow**: The flow generally moves from East Asia to the Middle East:

- China exported materials to Libya and Iraq for nuclear weapons and nerve gas
- Helped Algeria build a nuclear reactor suitable for weapons research
- Sold Iran nuclear technology U.S. officials believe could only be used for weapons
- Shipped components of 300-mile-range missiles to Pakistan
- North Korea sold advanced missiles and technology to Syria and Iran
- Some reverse flow: China received Stinger missiles from Pakistan

As Huntington describes it, this is "a renegades' mutual support pact, run by the proliferators and their backers." A new form of arms competition is occurring: one side develops arms while the other attempts not to balance but to limit and prevent that buildup while reducing its own capabilities.

## Implications for the West

Huntington's hypotheses suggest several policy implications:

**Short-term measures**:

- Promote greater cooperation and unity within Western civilization, particularly between European and North American components
- Incorporate into the West societies in Eastern Europe and Latin America whose cultures are close to the West
- Promote and maintain cooperative relations with Russia and Japan
- Prevent escalation of local inter-civilization conflicts into major inter-civilization wars
- Limit expansion of military strength of Confucian and Islamic states
- Moderate reduction of Western military capabilities and maintain superiority in East and Southwest Asia
- Exploit differences and conflicts among Confucian and Islamic states
- Support groups in other civilizations sympathetic to Western values and interests
- Strengthen international institutions that reflect Western interests and values

**Long-term measures**:

- Recognize that Western civilization is both Western and modern, while non-Western civilizations attempt to become modern without becoming Western
- Acknowledge that economic and military strength of non-Western civilizations relative to the West will increase
- Maintain economic and military power necessary to protect Western interests
- Develop profound understanding of basic religious and philosophical assumptions underlying other civilizations
- Identify elements of commonality between Western and other civilizations
- Accept that there will be no universal civilization, but instead a world of different civilizations, each of which must learn to coexist with the others

## Conclusion

Huntington concludes that for the immediate future, the central focus of conflict will be between the West and several Islamic-Confucian states. While he does not advocate the desirability of such conflicts, he argues these are plausible hypotheses about what the future may be like, and Western policy must be formulated accordingly. The paramount axis of world politics will be relations between "the West and the Rest," with civilization identity increasingly replacing ideological and other forms of identity as the dominant source of global conflict.
